## Applicability of LES for Estimating Collision Statistics of Particles in Homogeneous Turbulence under Two-way Momentum Coupling

Language: English.

**Co-authors**: Bogdan ROSA, Ahmad ABABAEI.

**Abstract**:

Accurate estimation of particle—particle collision stayisyics in turbulent flows requires high fidelity due to the complex interactions between the dispersed phase and the carrier turbulence, particularly at higher particle volume fractions. Consequently, the standard approach relies on direct numerical simulations (DNS) combined with Lagrangian tracking of individual particles. However, DNS remains computationally prohibitive: feasible grid sizes, and consequently attainable Reynolds numbers, are still limited (currently up to Re_λ = 531 [1], or Re_λ = 1300 for DNS without par*cles [2]). Many relevant applications, such as atmospheric cloud microphysics, involve systems that exhibit at least an order-of-magnitude higher Reynolds numbers. Moreover, for box grids, the maximal attainable Re_λ scales sublinearly with grid resolution (Re_λ ∝ N^(2/3)), while the total number of nodes scales as N³. Thus, DNS becomes increasingly non-scalable for such purposes.

This raises the question of whether large-eddy simulation (LES) can serve as a viable alternative for estimating collision statistics in turbulent particle-laden flows. By parameterizing the unresolved small scales, LES enables to achieve much higher eﬀective Reynolds numbers at substantially lower computational cost. On the other hand, replacing small-scale turbulence with a subgrid-scale model inevitably introduces uncertainties, particularly because preferential concentration and relative motion of low-inertia particles are predominantly governed by the smallest turbulent scales. Although earlier studies [3-5] have partially demonstrated feasibility of LES in this context, these investigations were carried out under one-way coupling that is valid for dilute systems, therefore limiting their applicability.

The objec*ve of the present study is to compare collision statistics of particles—including the radial distribution function (RDF) and radial relative velocity (RRV) at contact, as well as collision kernels—obtained from DNS and LES simulations under two-way momentum coupling. To facilitate such comparison, a single grid resolution is used per method (DNS: N = 256; LES: N = 64), chosen in such a way that both produce the same eﬀective Taylor-scale Reynolds number (Re_λ ≈ 165, matched within 0.6%). DNS results serve as the reference, and the primary question is whether LES reproduces the corresponding statistics with acceptable quantitative and qualitative accuracy.

Simulations were conducted using a massively parallel Eulerian–Lagrangian point-particle DNS solver. The fluid phase uses a pseudo-spectral method with periodic boundaries and deterministic large-scale forcing to maintain statistically sta*onary homogeneous isotropic turbulence. The LES variant uses the same solver but applies explicit spatial filtering and Smagorinsky subgrid-scale model with standard formula for spectral eddy viscosity following [6]. Particle trajectories are calculated individually according to the Maxey–Riley equations [7]. Simulations were performed both with and without gravity aﬀecting particle motion. For two-way coupling, particle–fluid momentum exchange is computed and distributed to the neighboring nodes of the Eulerian grid following approaches described in [8-11]. Between 0.5 and 20 million computational particles were used in simulations. Still, in some cases, to achieve higher mass loading of the disperse phase, each of them was set to represent more than one actual particle (super-droplet parameterization).

As expected, the introduction of small-scale modeling in LES resulted in discrepancies rela*ve to DNS. Under two-way coupling, for mass loadings smaller than 0.1, LES generally underestimates the RDF and overestimates the RRV, while for larger mass loadings this tendency is reversed. Interestingly, when combined into a collision kernel, the opposing biases largely cancel, particularly for water droplets of radius 30 μm (St ≈ 0.5), resulting in a close match. Also, LES aligns more closely with DNS in estimating RRV when gravity is considered, while the opposite is true for RDF, still resulting in even better match for collision kernels in both cases. Nevertheless, several configurations exhibit amplified errors due to small-scale filtering — most notably those involving large numbers of small, rapidly settling particles. These particles strongly modulate the smallest turbulent motions, which are precisely the scales removed by LES. Because the subgrid-scale model used here does not directly account for particle-induced turbulence modulation, it cannot reproduce these eﬀects, limiting indirect particle–particle interactions mediated by the flow. These shortcomings motivate future work in exploring particle-aware subgrid-scale models, such as [12].

In conclusion, LES emerges as a promising approach for estimating collision statistics in moderately dense particle-laden flows at higher Reynolds numbers, where two-way momentum coupling is important and DNS becomes computationally infeasible. At the same time, the study highlights specific limitations of classical subgrid-scale models in this context and points to the need for more advanced models that incorporate the dynamical influence of the dispersed phase. Notably, the collision kernel, a statistic that is key for applications, e.g. in models of cloud microphysics, is reproduced with surprisingly good accuracy due to compensating errors in RDF and RRV.
